Building with agents · draft 6

I talk to one session. I run a fleet that does the work.

I work from one chat window, four identities and a decision table with zero stops in it, and I report the one metric where my fleet loses. A mystery with numbers, and the resolution is not the model.

Sasha Demin · Provectus · 2026-11-09 · 9 min read

TL;DR: I carry less in my head than I did when I was the engineer, and the work got faster and better at the same time. The rest of this piece is how that is even possible, because the first time I saw the numbers I did not believe them either.

Every number carries its window; commands are in the appendix.

The claim

In the thirty days from 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-16 my GitHub login merged 169 of the 287 pull requests that landed on the project. The other four active engineers merged 118 between them, about 30 each. Of the code on the integration branch today, 66 percent of the lines are blamed to my login (279,451 of 423,540 across 1,794 code and doc files). My change-request rate from human reviewers over the 53-day audit window ending 2026-09-24 was 13 percent, the lowest of any frequent author; the team ranged from 25 to 40 percent.

The project is a parametric reinsurance agent that answers questions about rainfall and storms from a governed data lake. Real client, real reviewers, real merges. "Merged from my login" means a session running under my account opened the PR, I reviewed it, and I merged it; the typing was the agent's, the accountability mine. Opened in the same window: 206 of 336, median 166 changed lines against 67 to 609 for the others. Since 2026-08-01 the totals are 350 opened of 664.

So: one person, one chat window, more merged code than the other four combined, and the lowest rejection rate. What the hell is going on?

The first wrong answer

I first assumed I had a very good engineer in a box and that I type fast. I believed that for the first two months. It is wrong, and the way it is wrong is the key to everything else.

A very good engineer reads every file, writes every line, and holds every open thread in one head. When the engineer is a language model, that head is its context window. By lunch it is full of file dumps. Every turn re-sends all of it. The answers get slower, then vaguer, then wrong. I measured it: thirty days of transcripts (1,493 of them) showed the one window I talk to spending 63 percent of all tokens and 78 percent of output tokens. One assistant, however good, hits that wall every single day.

Dead end. So if it is not a better engineer, what is it?

Four identities, not one assistant

The session I talk to is not an engineer. It is a project manager, and I keep three peers running next to it all day:

  • Project Manager owns the board. It moves tickets, batches review asks, watches merges, and is the only identity allowed to send anything to a human.
  • Coordinator owns the live work. It cuts a goal into lanes, assigns each lane one owner and one acceptance set, and merges them in the right order.
  • Teacher owns the harness itself. Every correction I make, every reviewer finding, every flaky test becomes a mechanism in the rules, with telemetry to prove it fires.
  • Researcher owns the evidence. When a decision needs a source, it reads the source in full and posts what it found on the ticket.

Under those four I run Directors, one per topic, each with its own worktree so two writers never touch the same tree. Under the Directors I run the cheap workers: a Sonnet model for a bounded build, a swarm of Haiku models when the job is "check these forty rows" rather than "think". A worker reads the same files the engineer-in-a-box would have read, returns a summary of 1,500 tokens, and its context dies. That is the trick that beats the wall: the expensive context never accumulates in the window I talk to.

And here is the first surprise from our own dispatch data: Haiku judges were accepted 83 percent of the time (n=24) and the frontier model 45 percent (n=11), where accepted means dispatched once, not re-dispatched, and not followed by a correction from me. Small samples, one direction: a judge needs an independent frame more than it needs a bigger brain. The cheapest model wins the judging job on merit.

Fine. Four identities, a ladder of cheap workers. But that raises the real question. If I write forty sentences a day, who is making the hundreds of decisions a project needs?

How a decision gets made without me

I am asked most about the part with the least magic, and my rule there is: assume and disclose.

I went back and read sixty-five of my own answers to agent questions over one week. I expected a mix. What I found was a table that already existed in my head and that I had never written down. Nineteen answers were "yes, as proposed". Eighteen were "push forward". Sixteen were an override I could have stated as a rule in advance. The number of times I said "stop and wait" was zero.

Zero. Every question that interrupted me that week was a question I would have answered the same way without being asked. So I wrote the table down. Now my agent reads the row, does the default, and writes one line in its report: assumed X, reverses if Y. A short list still stops my fleet: pushing to production without a directive, sending a message to a person who was not named in the ask, money, secrets, anything irreversible. Everything else proceeds.

That should scare you a little, and it scares me. An agent that never asks is an agent that ships whatever it believes. What stops it from shipping a wrong number?

Key takeaways
  1. Workers read the files and report in 1,500 tokens; the window I talk to never fills up.
  2. Write down the answers you would give anyway. A week of 65 answers held zero "stop and wait".
  3. The model proposes; code owns every number and every gate, and those gates fail closed.

The model never touches the gate

My second rule is the one that makes the first one safe: I let the model propose and explain, and I let deterministic code own every number and every gate.

I merge through a script that reads the branch protection, the review state, and the check results, not through a model's opinion that it looks fine. I move a ticket only on an executed command quoted in the comment. A hook rejects my PR body before it reaches GitHub if it names nothing it actually ran. These gates fail closed. Interpretation (which period did the user mean, which city) is allowed to assume, but it must say so.

So the chain every pull request walks, with no exceptions for mine:

  1. Plan, then critique the plan. A Director writes the brief; a cheap critic cuts scope and names what would refute the plan before anyone builds.
  2. Red test first. The builder writes one failing test, watches it fail, then writes the shortest diff that turns it green.
  3. Two reviews by different models. A full review with several lenses (correctness, contracts, silent failures, tests), then a separate bug hunt on the diff by a model that did not write it. Findings get fixed before the PR opens, not after.
  4. Two runs on two heads. Anything touching the agent runs twice on the candidate runtime and twice on the current one, and a row counts as a change only if it is stable on both. Then the same questions go through the real Slack workspace, twice.
  5. A script merges, not a model. It reads the branch rules, the human review state and the checks, and refuses when any of them is red. A human approval is still required on this repo; my fleet cannot approve its own work.
  6. Re-plan when a gate fails. A red golden row goes back to a bug hunt on the class of failure, not a patch on the quoted line, because the patch is what gets rejected the second time.

Does any of that actually happen, or is it a nice diagram? Over the last thirty days, 123 of the 182 pull requests I merged name one of those executed gates in the body: a full review, a bug hunt, a candidate battery or a golden run. For the 125 merged by the rest of the team it is 18. That is not a quality verdict on anyone; it is a count of what was written down, and it is the thing I would show first.

Research runs the same way. A Researcher lane reads sources in full before a design decision and posts what it found on the ticket; 28 of the 41 research notes in the project knowledge base were written in October, under the coordination model.

At this point the story is too clean. Lowest rejection rate, most merges, every gate in code. If you have shipped software you are waiting for the catch. There is one, and my own ledger found it before any reviewer did.

The catch

Over the audited 53 days my functions averaged 19 lines against 24 for the two next-most-active authors, and my duplicate-block rate was three to nine per thousand lines against eighteen to thirty-five. Good. Then the third metric came in.

My cyclomatic complexity was the highest on the team: 4.96 mean against 2.9 to 4.3, with four functions above 40. That is the one place I sit outside the band. It splits by domain (agent runtime versus infrastructure), so it is partly what I work on, but it is real, and a fleet that writes small, non-duplicated, deeply branching functions is a fleet optimising for the two metrics it can see. It is now a review lens, which is the Teacher's job: a finding becomes a mechanism, not a memory.

Two more things go wrong often enough to name. Live batteries flip, meaning two identical runs on the same image choose different tool inputs, about three percent of the time on direct invoke and over twenty percent through the chat bridge, so every behavioural claim needs two runs on both heads before it counts. And the laptop itself is the bottleneck more often than the models: with six lanes live, load sits above twice the core count and the spawn gate refuses new work until it clears.

What I actually get out of this

I come back to the one line at the top. Less in my head: I write forty sentences a day and read reports of 1,500 tokens, not files, because the decision table answers the questions that used to interrupt me and the gates answer the ones I used to lose sleep over. Faster: 206 pull requests in thirty days, median 166 lines, each one through the same chain. Better: the lowest change-request rate on the team, two reviews and two live runs before anything merges, and my one weak metric found by my own ledger rather than by a reviewer.

The three go together because of one thing. It was never the model. It was writing down what I would have said, and putting everything I would have checked into code that cannot be talked out of it.

Every number above has a command next to it. To check the first one, run the pull-request listing for the repository with a merged-after filter of 2026-09-16 and group by author; it takes four seconds. That is why I trust the numbers enough to publish them, and why the complexity number is in here next to the good ones.
